Every manager looking for software to make a department's work easier eventually faces the same question: is there a ready-made product on the market, or should we build software tailored to our organization?

The answer usually lies not in the budget spreadsheet but in the processes themselves. In this guide we look at when each option makes sense, the costs that comparisons tend to leave out, and the questions that will sharpen your decision.

## The short answer: there is no single right choice, only the right question

Off-the-shelf and custom software are not rivals; they solve different problems. Off-the-shelf software is designed to meet a need shared by many companies in a standard way. Custom software is shaped around your processes, your data and your rules alone.

So the real question is not "which one is better?" but this: **Is this a process that sets our organization apart, or one that everyone runs the same way?**

## When is off-the-shelf software the right choice?

Off-the-shelf software, meaning packaged products or cloud products sold by subscription, is usually the most sensible choice when:

- **The process is an industry standard.** In areas such as accounting, payroll, corporate e-mail or basic customer relationship management, the way work is done hardly changes from one company to the next.
- **The process gives you no competitive advantage.** Calculating payroll differently from your competitors will not win you customers.
- **You need to start quickly.** A ready-made product can be in use within days.
- **Your process can adapt to the product.** If the workflow the product suggests is good enough for you, adopting it is often the most economical path.

In these cases there is no point in reinventing the wheel. A good packaged product offers a solution that has matured over years of feedback from thousands of users.

## When is custom software the right choice?

Custom software comes to the fore when these signs appear:

- **The process is specific to your organization.** Your approval chains, handovers between departments or data structure do not fit a standard product.
- **You need to connect several systems.** Data is moved by hand between the HR system, the intranet, the ERP and e-mail.
- **Regulation and audit trails are critical.** In regulated industries such as banking, energy and healthcare, you need to be able to trace who approved what and when.
- **The packaged product is only half used.** If Excel files, e-mail threads and manual reminders still revolve around licensed software, the product is not really solving the process.
- **The process is part of your competitive advantage.** A workflow that lets you respond to customers faster or operate at lower cost deserves to be your own.

## Side by side

| Criterion | Off-the-shelf software | Custom software |
|---|---|---|
| Fit with your process | You adapt to the product | The software adapts to your process |
| Initial investment | Low | Higher |
| Time to launch | Days or weeks | Weeks or months |
| Licence cost | Recurring, per user and per year | None; annual maintenance applies |
| Integration | As far as the product allows | As far as you need |
| Ownership of source code and data | With the vendor | With your organization |
| Adapting to change | Depends on the vendor's roadmap | Depends on your own priorities |
| Vendor lock-in | High | Low; the code is yours |

This table alone is not enough to make a decision. But it helps you see which criteria weigh most for you.

## The overlooked cost: the Excel around the package

The most common mistake in off-the-shelf comparisons is to look only at the licence fee. The steps the product cannot handle do not disappear; they land back on employees' desks.

Consider an example: an events platform collects registration forms but cannot match employee details with the organization's HR system. So after every event someone exports the list to Excel, matches employee numbers and sends certificates one by one. The licence fee shows up on the invoice; those hours do not.

To account for this hidden cost, look at:

- Employee hours spent on steps outside the product
- The cost of fixing errors made while moving data by hand
- Licence fees that grow as the number of users grows
- Customization and consulting fees paid to fit the product to your process
- The switching cost if the vendor changes its pricing or product policy

## Calculating total cost of ownership

The right comparison uses the total cost over three to five years, not the first year. A simple framework:

**For off-the-shelf software:** licences (users × years) + customization and integration + training + the annual cost of the manual work the product cannot handle.

**For custom software:** development investment + annual maintenance and support + the annual cost of the manual work that remains after the software.

A real example: in a bank's HR department, the annual operating cost of the time two employees spent managing events and training was **TRY 565,820**. With a custom intranet platform, this fell to **TRY 132,185** a year. The **TRY 350,000** software investment paid for itself in about 10 months thanks to annual savings of about **TRY 433,635**. In the following years, the savings went straight to the bottom line.

## Eight questions to decide

Answer these questions honestly with your team. The more "yes" answers you have, the stronger the case for custom software.

1. Is this process a way of working that sets us apart from our competitors?
2. When we tried packaged products, were there steps we could not use without changing our process?
3. Do Excel files, e-mails and manual reminders still revolve around the current solution?
4. Is data moved by hand between several systems in this process?
5. Are audit trails, approval history or regulatory compliance critical?
6. Will licence costs grow significantly as the number of users grows?
7. Does it matter that our data and the source code stay with us?
8. Will this process change in the coming years, requiring the software to adapt quickly?

## A third way: the hybrid approach

For most organizations, the realistic answer is to use both. Core systems such as accounting and ERP stay on packaged products, while department-specific workflows are handled by a custom layer that talks to those systems.

In this approach, custom software does not replace your existing systems. It fills the gaps between them: connecting data, automating approval flows and bringing reporting into one place. You benefit from both the maturity of packaged products and the flexibility of custom software.

## How has AI changed this equation?

Until a few years ago, custom software was often an option only for large organizations because of its high budget and long delivery times. AI assisted development tools have lowered this threshold significantly by allowing experienced engineers to deliver the same work in less time. Today, custom software for a department-scale process has become an investment comparable to an off-the-shelf product for many organizations.

## Conclusion

> If the process is standard and you can adapt to the product, off-the-shelf software is usually the right choice. If the process is specific to your organization, connects several systems, or still has manual work revolving around a packaged product, custom software is the more economical and efficient solution in the long run.
>
> Base the decision not on the licence fee but on the three to five year total cost of ownership and the real workload in the process.

If you are not sure which option suits you, start by measuring the process. A proper analysis often answers the question on its own.
